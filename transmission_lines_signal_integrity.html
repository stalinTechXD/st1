1. Why should you even care?

In basic circuits you learn a wire is just a wire — voltage is the same everywhere on it, instantly. That is a lie that works only at low speed.

When your signal changes fast (fast edges / high frequency), electricity does not reach the other end instantly. The signal travels as a wave, roughly at half the speed of light on a PCB (~15 cm/ns). If the wire is "electrically long" compared to how fast your signal switches, that wave can bounce back, distort your data, and cause bit errors.

The one rule that starts everything:
Treat a wire as a transmission line when the signal's rise time is comparable to the round-trip travel time. Rule of thumb: if the trace length > rise_time × velocity / 6, you MUST think about transmission lines.
🌊 Analogy: Shout in a small room → you hear yourself once, clean. Shout in a canyon → echoes bounce back and garble the next word. A short trace = small room. A fast signal on a long trace = canyon full of echoes. Signal integrity is the art of killing the echoes.

2. What actually IS a transmission line?

A transmission line is any pair of conductors that carries a signal and its return current: a PCB trace over a ground plane, a coax cable, a twisted pair. It is not special hardware — it is a way of modeling a normal wire once speed matters.

We model even a "simple" trace as an infinite ladder of tiny inductors (L), capacitors (C), and small resistances. This is the RLGC model:

Series L (inductance of the wire) Shunt C (capacitance to return path)
Every tiny segment adds a little series inductance and a little capacitance to ground. That distributed L and C is what forces the signal to travel as a wave — and it sets the line's characteristic impedance.

3. Characteristic Impedance Z₀ — the heart of it all

As a wave rolls down the line, at every instant it "sees" the ratio of voltage to current. For an ideal line that ratio is a constant called characteristic impedance Z₀. It is NOT resistance — it does not burn power — it is just the ratio the wave feels while moving.

Z₀ = √( L / C ) L = inductance per unit length, C = capacitance per unit length
🚰 Analogy: Z₀ is like the "thickness" of a pipe as felt by the water wave. It depends on the geometry (trace width, distance to ground, dielectric), not on how long the pipe is. Common values: 50 Ω (most single-ended digital/RF), 75 Ω (video/coax), 90–100 Ω (USB/differential pairs).

Interactive: change the geometry and watch Z₀ change (simplified microstrip model).

Notice: wider trace or closer ground → more capacitance → lower Z₀. Thicker dielectric → higher Z₀.

4. Reflections — the villain of signal integrity

The wave travels happily at Z₀ until it hits a point where the impedance changes (the end of the line, a connector, a via). At that discontinuity, part of the energy reflects back, like an echo.

How much reflects is given by the reflection coefficient Γ (gamma):

Γ = ( ZLoad − Z₀ ) / ( ZLoad + Z₀ ) Γ ranges from −1 (short) to +1 (open). Γ = 0 means NO reflection = perfect.
SituationZLoadΓMeaning
Matched (perfect)= Z₀0No echo 🎉
Open circuit∞+1Full echo, same sign
Short circuit0−1Full echo, flipped
Under-terminated< Z₀negativePartial flipped echo
Over-terminated> Z₀positivePartial echo (overshoot)
Reflections cause the ugly stuff you see on a scope: overshoot, undershoot, ringing, and stair-steps. That is what corrupts your logic levels and timing. Kill reflections → clean signal.

6. How engineers fix it: Termination

Termination = deliberately adding resistors so the impedance is matched and Γ → 0. The main styles:

Series (Source) Termination

Put a resistor Rs at the driver so Rs + Zdriver = Z₀. The reflection comes back and is absorbed at the source. Cheap, low power, great for point-to-point. Very common in FPGAs/CPUs.

Low power One receiver only

Parallel (End) Termination

Put a resistor = Z₀ to ground (or Vtt) at the receiver. Kills the reflection immediately at the load. Great for buses / multi-drop, but burns DC power.

Clean for buses Burns power
  • Thevenin termination — two resistors to VCC and GND, sets a bias level; used in DDR/SSTL.
  • AC termination — resistor + capacitor in series, blocks DC to save power.
  • Differential termination — a single resistor across the pair (e.g. 100 Ω) for USB/Ethernet/LVDS.
Golden goal of signal integrity routing: keep Z₀ constant everywhere (no width changes, smooth returns, few vias) AND terminate the ends so reflections die. That's 90% of the job.
